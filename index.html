How this is drawn

Directions to the Moon and Sun at 00:00 UTC on this date, calculated from NASA/JPL Horizons vectors and turned to face the globe by our own calculation (IAU 2006 precession and sidereal time; small effects such as nutation and polar motion are left out). Not observed.

Earth and Moon are drawn to scale: true sizes and true distance. The Sun is drawn at 200 Earth radii instead of about 23,000, along its calculated direction, and resized so that, as seen from Earth's centre, it covers the same angle as the real Sun. How big it looks on your screen depends on where the camera is.

The globe's layers show their own times, so they are hidden here. The Earth imagery keeps its own date. Full details on Sources & Details →

    Search scope & limitations

    This searches only the records your browser has already loaded from each layer's latest snapshot. It makes no request of any kind. It is not a search of the world, of history, or of everything a source holds.

    Earthquake descriptions are searched exactly as USGS writes them. Vessel names and MMSIs are searched as received. Fire detections and forest disturbance publish no text, so they are never text-searched; fire detections do take part in a coordinate search.

    There is no geocoder and no country boundaries. A place or country word matches the source's own description text: it finds records described that way and misses records in the same country described some other way.

    A coordinate search always offers to move the view to that position, and separately lists loaded records within 250 km. That radius is a choice made for this box — it is not a coverage boundary.

    A hidden, unknown, or withheld layer is not searched, and the panel names it. A result is tied to the collector run, layer visibility and freshness it was found under; if any of those change the result is marked and refused rather than opened.

    Day/night Computed
    Current sunlight geometry—not observation coverage.
    What this is and is not

    Calculated solar illumination for the time shown, from the clock — computed, not observed.

    It is not a record of whether anything was observed in daylight. The records on this globe were observed at many different times, not at the time shown.

    It is not satellite coverage, not cloud, not visibility, and not evidence of absence.

    It does not explain fire-detection counts. VIIRS detects thermal anomalies by day and by night, and each detection carries its own day/night flag.

    The imagery beneath this shading is dated imagery, not live, and its date is shown separately; every record carries its own observation time. Three different clocks, kept apart.

    Geometric boundary only: the Sun's centre on the horizon, with no refraction and no solar disc, so this is not sunrise or sunset. Twilight is not shown.

    Ocean heat Analysed
    Sea surface temperature and its difference from NOAA's 1991–2020 normal for this day of year. Satellite-based, with gaps filled by NOAA. A dated snapshot, not live.
    What this is and is not

    One day, 9 Nov 2026 (UTC), from NOAA Coral Reef Watch: CoralTemp v3.1 sea surface temperature and its difference from NOAA's 1991–2020 normal for that location and day of year. Tap the water to read both.

    Analysed, not observed: a satellite-based estimate with gaps filled by NOAA's analysis. It describes the surface of a grid cell, can differ from a nearby beach or buoy, and says nothing about the water below.

    One warm snapshot does not identify a marine heatwave or coral bleaching. Sea ice, and places with no 1991–2020 normal, are shown as no data — never as zero.

    Coastlines follow NOAA's 5 km land mask; features narrower than a cell can read as water.

    Orbital objects Computed
    calculated from published orbital elements — not observed; markers enlarged, not to scale
    Computed positions of catalogued objects in orbit, from published orbital elements—not observed. Each layer is off until you switch it on.
    What this is and is not

    Each marker is a position computed (SGP4) from the latest general-perturbations element set Space-Track.org publishes for that object: every catalogued object not yet decayed whose elements are under 10 days old. Older elements are filtered out upstream and are not in these counts. Nothing here was observed at the time shown.

    The layers follow Space-Track's object type: payloads, rocket bodies, debris. Objects of unknown type are counted, not drawn. Whether a payload is working is not established here: operational status is not used yet, so no payload is shown as active or inactive.

    It shows the scale and distribution of the public catalogue. It is not a measure of how much sky is obscured, of ground coverage, or of collision risk, and it is not every object in orbit: classified objects and objects too small to track are not in the catalogue.

    Markers are enlarged so they can be seen; they are not to physical scale. Positions are drawn at their propagated height above the surface.

    Element sets age. Objects whose elements are more than three days old at the time shown are counted on screen; three days is a disclosure threshold, not an accuracy limit. Objects above geostationary height (35,786 km) are counted too.

    Horizons calculates these distances. Mother Earth shows a dated daily sample from an export, refreshed by hand.
    Details

    Geometric distance from the centre of the Earth to the centre of the Moon or the Sun, one sample a day at 00:00 UTC, calculated by NASA/JPL Horizons (DE441). Not an observation.

    "Today" is the UTC date on your device's clock. A day without its own sample shows as unavailable; a neighbouring day is never shown in its place.

    Direction comes from Horizons' range rate at 00:00 UTC: negative means getting closer at that moment, positive means getting farther.

    Parts of each chart after today are calculated ahead. Shaded days lie outside the calculated range of this export.

    Measured Lunar recession

    The Moon's mean distance from Earth grows by about 3.8 cm a year, measured by timing laser pulses to reflectors left on the Moon. Tides slow Earth's spin and move the Moon slowly outward; at the current rate days lengthen by about 2.3 milliseconds a century.

    NASA's eclipse pages estimate that, hundreds of millions of years from now, the Moon will look too small to cover the Sun fully, ending total solar eclipses.

    Estimated Widening from solar mass loss

    Earth's orbit widens by roughly a centimetre a year at today's rate — estimated from changes in Mercury's orbit consistent with solar mass loss. The Sun loses mass as it radiates energy and through the solar wind.

    That widening hardly matters for climate on human timescales. Over billions of years the ageing Sun sends Earth more energy, which matters far more: Earth can receive more energy even as its orbit widens.

    Over a year, Earth's distance from the Sun changes by about five million kilometres. Solar mass loss would widen Earth's orbit by roughly one metre over a century at today's rate.
