What LRO measured: brightness temperature around local noon Measured

No valid data in this window

Withheld by our display limit: poleward of 70° for noon, 80° for midnight

Bolometric brightness temperature is an equivalent blackbody temperature: it represents the thermal radiation Diviner measured, summed over wavelength. It is an effective radiative temperature. Where rock and soil, or sunlit and shaded slopes, share a cell, it is not the physical temperature of any one surface. Noon and midnight are each place’s own local time, not the Sun’s position now.

Differently processed Diviner maps can give different values, especially at sharp warm features. See Sources for our comparison.

The readout gives the number of quarter-hour bins used and, for two or more, the range between them: a spread, not an uncertainty. Values are rounded to 0.1 K; the rounding is not the accuracy.

Lighting uses the current UTC minute. The LRO path is a dated example from 10–11 June 2026.

View

Use Zoom in and Zoom out, or pinch on supported devices, to look closer: the Moon grows inside its box. The appearance picture holds about 5 km to a pixel at the equator, so it blurs up close. The temperature map’s cells are 0.5° (about 15 km north–south and at the equator, narrower east–west towards the poles). Opens evenly lit so the whole surface can be seen: that is not how the Moon is lit today. “Lit as now” shades it from the calculated direction of the Sun at this device’s current UTC minute. “Show height (LOLA)” replaces the picture with a colour map of measured height (2009–2016 laser data) and switches sunlight off while it is shown. “Show temperature (Diviner)” replaces it with a map of brightness temperature around each place’s own local noon or midnight (2009–2015 data), also with sunlight off. Only one map is shown at a time. Appearance map from NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio, made from Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter data: tuned for looks, not measurement. Poleward of 70° it is filled from a lower-resolution map. “Show a detailed site: Apollo 11 (LROC)” turns the globe to the Apollo 11 landing site, marks it with a ring and shows one dated LRO camera picture of it below, at two scales. The globe itself stays at about 5 km a pixel. Drawn to fill the view. Sizes are not comparable between the Earth, Moon and Sun pages. The globe and the spacecraft drawing below are separate views. “Show LRO’s dated path” puts the 10–11 June 2026 example on the globe, at the time chosen in the drawing; no current position is shown.

Mission on this page

  • NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has circled the Moon since 2009 in a low orbit that passes over both poles. Its laser altimeter measures the height of the lunar surface along each pass, building a map of the Moon’s topography.

    Mission status: “It is now conducting an extended mission as a communication relay/south pole observing in a low-maintenance orbit.” Source: NASA/JPL Horizons data sheet for LRO, revised 7 Oct 2026; checked 13 Nov 2026.

Sources and method

  • Dated example (10–11 June 2026): NASA/GSFC Flight Dynamics Facility definitive trajectory, NASA PDS data set LRO-L-SPICE-6-V1.0, product lrorg_2026074_2026166_v01.bsp (label: “Merged SPICE SPK file containing Regular Definitive LRO Spacecraft trajectory.”). The kernel’s own one-minute states for that day are shown as written, in the J2000 frame centred on the Moon; no interpolation. Positions rounded to 1 m. Archive at NAIF
  • Check: at 11:45, 12:00 and 12:15 UTC on 10 June 2026 the kernel states agree with a fresh NASA/JPL Horizons evaluation to about 2 cm.
  • Today: no position is shown. The published prediction for LRO has not been compared with a later definitive trajectory; a study of that is running and publishes nothing.
  • Moon globe: colour map lroc_color_2k.jpg (2025 version) from NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio, “CGI Moon Kit”. Our copy is served from this site, unchanged. Credit: NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio.
  • On the globe: the same one-minute states, each turned once into the Moon’s mean Earth frame (MOON_ME_DE440_ME421) at its own time, with NAIF’s SPICE toolkit and JPL’s DE440 Moon orientation. Checked against our own evaluation of DE440 and against NASA/JPL Horizons. Altitude is above a sphere of 1,737.4 km, not above the terrain. Details
  • Lit as now: calculated, not observed. The directions of the Sun and the Earth over the Moon come from a table we built with NAIF’s SPICE toolkit from JPL’s DE440 positions and Moon orientation, every six hours from 1 June 2026 to 1 January 2028 UTC, in the same frame as the map; the page interpolates between entries (within 0.005° of the direct calculation at every midpoint). Checked against our own evaluation of DE440 and against NASA/JPL Horizons. The time is this device’s clock. A smooth sphere and a point Sun: no relief shadows, no earthshine, no eclipses. The night side is shown faintly as a display aid. Details
  • Height (optional): LOLA gridded topography LDEM_4_FLOAT, product version V3.0, data set LRO-L-LOLA-4-GDR-V1.0 (PDS4 urn:nasa:pds:lro_lola_rdr:data_gridded:ldem_4_float::1.0), LOLA Science Team, NASA GSFC; bundle DOI 10.17189/tnyt-7007 (Smith & Neumann 2023). Laser data from 13 July 2009 to 29 November 2016, height relative to a 1,737.4 km sphere on a 0.25° grid. PDS data are in the public domain. We round each cell to whole metres and draw one colour per cell; values north of 89.5°N are withheld because the float and integer versions of this grid disagree there by up to 1.4 km. Details.
  • Temperature (optional): Diviner Global Cumulative Product, data set LRO-L-DLRE-5-GCP-V1.0, field tbol (“Bolometric average calibrated brightness temperature”), Diviner team, UCLA; bundle DOI 10.17189/wj0s-w188 (Paige, Sullivan & Williams 2022). Nadir observations from 5 July 2009 to 1 April 2015, binned by the team into 0.5° cells and quarter hours of local time from the mean radiances of channels 3–9. PDS data are in the public domain. Shown here: our unweighted mean of the available bins between 11:30 and 12:30, or 23:30 and 00:30, local time, with the number of bins and the range between them. Poleward of 70° (noon) and 80° (midnight) the map is withheld by our display policy. Fixed colour scales: noon 281–395 K, midnight 70–105 K (the 1st and 99th percentiles of the shown grid cells, rounded outward to whole kelvin; shares are of 0.5° grid cells, not of surface area). General meaning of bolometric temperature: Powell et al. 2023, JGR Planets 128, e2022JE007532, §2.5. Comparison: at ten selected high-contrast midnight sites, this map’s cell values differed from a separately processed high-resolution Diviner midnight mosaic by −1.9 to +16.7 K (median +8.7 K); ten background sites differed by less than 1 K. The products use different processing, sampling and periods; the causes of the differences have not been isolated. This comparison is not an accuracy estimate. Details.
  • Detailed site (optional): LROC Narrow Angle Camera orthophotos of the Apollo 11 landing site, products NAC_DTM_APOLLO11_M150368601_50CM and NAC_DTM_APOLLO11_M150368601_2M, data set LRO-L-LROC-5-RDR-V1.0 in the NASA PDS (Imaging Node), from one observation, M150368601, on 22 January 2011, 20:48:53–20:49:11 UTC. PDS data products are in the public domain (LROC’s terms). Credit: NASA/GSFC/Arizona State University. Shown: a 512 m square at the product’s 0.5 m a pixel, and an area about 4.2 km across averaged from 2 m to 4 m a pixel (no-data blocks hatched); both stretched for display, not a measured reflectance. The ring on the globe marks the published position of the Lunar Module descent stage (LROC’s 2016 list), not the picture’s footprint. Details
  • Moon radius: 1,737.4 km (NASA/JPL Horizons, “Radius (IAU)”).
