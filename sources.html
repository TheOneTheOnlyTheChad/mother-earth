Coverage grades, evidence and unknown coverage

Every map cell carries an activity count and, separately, a coverage grade with an evidence string. adequate: the collector observed throughout the window and the source documents coverage of that cell (source coverage can still be incomplete). partial: records observed; coverage incomplete — the count is not a total, and not a guaranteed minimum of real-world activity. unknown: nothing can be said about the cell. A cell with no outline has no records; that is not evidence of absence unless it was adequately observed. Comparisons (phase 3) will run only on adequate cells.

Day/night overlay — computed astronomical context

An optional overlay on World View, off unless you turn it on, shading the part of the Earth where the Sun is below the horizon at the time it states. It is the first thing on this site that is computed rather than observed, and it is classified that way: computed (astronomical context — calculated from the clock, not observed). It is not a layer. It has no source, no snapshot, no collector, no coverage grade and no freshness, and it never appears in the status report, which is a statement about observations.

What it shows. Calculated solar illumination for the UTC time displayed beside the toggle and in the legend. Nothing more.

What it does not show. It is not a record of whether anything was observed in daylight — the records on the globe were observed at many different times, not at the time shown. It is not satellite coverage, not cloud, not visibility, and not evidence of absence. It does not explain fire-detection counts: VIIRS detects thermal anomalies by day and by night, each detection carries its own day/night flag, and the low counts early in a UTC day come from the FIRMS daily reset and near-real-time lag, not from darkness. Three clocks stay separate on this page: this overlay's calculation time, the imagery date (dated imagery, not live), and each record's own observation time.

Method and assumptions. The subsolar point is computed from the Astronomical Almanac / USNO low-precision solar coordinates — mean longitude and anomaly, a two-term equation of centre, obliquity, then right ascension and declination with Greenwich mean sidereal time for the hour angle. That formulation is a US Government work; no third-party code is used at runtime and the page gains no dependency for it. The boundary drawn is geometric: the Sun's centre on the horizon, with no atmospheric refraction (~34′) and no solar semi-diameter (~16′), so true sunrise and sunset lie about 50′ of arc away — further where the Sun's path is shallow. This line is not sunrise. Twilight bands are not drawn. A spherical Earth is assumed: no topography, no observer elevation. The time comes from your own device's clock.

Validation. Checked against astronomy-engine (MIT), a VSOP87-based ephemeris validated against JPL, used as a test-only dependency and never shipped. Over 2024-01-01 to 2030-01-01, 7,516 samples at 7-hour steps, the angular separation between the two subsolar directions was mean 0.0040°, P99 0.0103°, maximum 0.0124°. Compared like-for-like against the reference's geometric solar altitude over 10,080 points spanning both equinoxes and both solstices, there were 0 disagreements. This is agreement with one reference over a stated range, not a claim of absolute accuracy, and it says nothing about how closely the line matches an observed sunrise — see the assumptions above.

Those figures describe the arithmetic only. What you see on the globe is coarser, for three separate and additive reasons.

LimitSizeWhat it is
Solar position≤ 0.0124°Agreement of the computed subsolar point with the reference. The arithmetic.
Drawing resolution~0.35°The shading is painted into a 1024 × 512 texture, so the boundary is quantised to about a third of a degree per pixel.
Update intervalup to 0.25°It recomputes once a minute, and the Earth turns 0.25° of longitude in that time, so the line can be up to a minute stale.

These are three different kinds of statement and are deliberately not added together: the first is a measured agreement, the second a quantisation bound, the third a drift bound. Combining them would assert a figure nobody measured. Taken separately: the computed position agrees with the reference to within 0.0124°; the texture quantises the shading to about 0.35°; and the line drifts by up to 0.25° between repaints. Only the first is a measurement. Separately again, the geometric boundary is about 0.83° from an observed sunrise, for the refraction and solar-disc reasons above. None of this is a reason to read the line as sunrise.

A dependency on library internals. The overlay draws by adding its own sphere to the scene graph of globe.gl, and it obtains the three.js constructors it needs by reading them off objects that library has already created — there is no global THREE to use. That is reliance on another library's internals, so the version is pinned (currently 2.45.0) and the automated checks assert that the pinned version under test is the one the page loads. It also copies the globe mesh's own rotation and sphere parameters rather than assuming how the texture lines up with longitude. If a future version stops exposing what is needed, the overlay refuses to switch on, resets its toggle and says so, and the globe, its records, picking and search are left exactly as they were.

Cost. None. It makes no request, has no collector and no backend, and recomputes locally once a minute while the page is visible, and immediately when you enable it, return to the tab, or restore the page.

Drifting buoys — latest reported fixes (observation)

Each point is one drifting buoy at its own latest reported fix in the 24 hours before the hub's run. The fixes were taken at different times, so the layer is not a picture of one moment; every buoy's inspector shows its own fix time. Where a buoy is now is unknown.

Source: NOAA Observing System Monitoring Center (OSMC) ERDDAP, dataset OSMC_flattened — observations relayed over the WMO Global Telecommunication System (GTS); platform types "DRIFTING BUOYS" and "DRIFTING BUOYS (GENERIC)". Licence stated in the dataset: CC0 1.0 (public domain dedication). These are real-time reports, not the Global Drifter Program's quality-controlled data, which are published years later.

Collection: the hub makes one request each hour (about 100 KB, about 1,300 buoys, measured 3 Nov 2026) and publishes one current snapshot, which replaces the previous one on the globe. What the hub retains beyond that is stated, from the hub's own report, in What Mother Earth keeps. Before anything is shown it checks the header and units, each row's time, position and buoy id, and that the count has not collapsed (at least 200 buoys, and at least half the previous count). A report stamped in the future (10 buoys on 3 Nov 2026) is refused and counted, never drawn. If a run fails, the previous snapshot stays with its own times and the layer goes Unknown after 4 hours.

Sea-surface temperature is shown as the buoy reported it (°C), in each buoy's inspector and as the colour of its dot on the globe: eight 5 °C classes from below 0 to 30 °C and above, on a scale that runs from dark blue (cold) to yellow (warm). A grey dot means temperature unavailable: either the buoy reported none, or the value was outside −3 to 40 °C and the hub withheld it (it is counted, never shown as zero). The colour is the temperature at that buoy's own latest fix, so neighbouring dots can be hours apart, and it is not compared with any normal. Mother Earth applies a range check. It performs no further quality checks on the reported temperature.

Coverage: buoys are few and far apart. A cell with no buoy says nothing about that water. The layer is off when the page loads and is fetched only while it is switched on.

Ocean heat — dated snapshot (analysed, optional layer)

What it is. One day, 9 Nov 2026 (UTC): sea surface temperature from NOAA Coral Reef Watch's CoralTemp v3.1, and its difference from NOAA's 1991–2020 normal for that location and day of year (NOAA's SST anomaly product, 1991–2020 baseline, version 3.1-clim19912020-v1). The colour shows the difference; a tap shows the temperature, the difference and the date. It is a dated snapshot, not live, and nothing refreshes it.

Analysed, not observed. CoralTemp is a gap-free blend of gridded satellite analyses (night-time observations, calibrated to 0.2 m depth); where cloud hid the sea, NOAA's analysis fills the gap. The files carry no per-cell flag saying which values were filled, so no tap is labelled observed or filled. Each value is an estimate for the surface of a grid cell and can differ from a nearby beach or buoy reading. It says nothing about the water below the surface, and one warm snapshot does not identify a marine heatwave or coral bleaching. NOAA notes anomalies are less reliable at high latitudes.

The normal. NOAA's daily 1991–2020 climatology: for each calendar day, the mean of that day's 30 values over 1991–2020. NOAA's coral heat-stress products use a different, older baseline (1985–1990 plus 1993); this layer does not. Where sea ice was present during 1991–2020, NOAA publishes no normal, and the layer says so.

What is drawn. NOAA's 5 km (0.05°) grid is averaged into squares (0.25° or 0.5°, stated on the card): each square's value is the mean of its water cells only — never land, ice or cells without a normal, and never filled from neighbours. Where it is drawn follows NOAA's own 5 km mask: land stays clear, and sea ice or "no 1991–2020 normal" is a grey checked fill, never zero. Coastlines therefore follow NOAA's 5 km mask; features narrower than a cell can read as water. Colours: nine classes, blue for colder and orange for warmer than normal; −0.2 to +0.2 °C is shown as near normal, as on NOAA's own maps.

Checks before anything is drawn. The page holds the snapshot's manifest fingerprint (SHA-256) and the manifest holds every data file's; a mismatch draws nothing. The files are served by Mother Earth's hub from a fixed, immutable copy, which also checks each file's size and fingerprint before sending it; the layer is off when the page opens and fetched only while it is switched on. The snapshot generator stops on any change in the inputs or their structure (input fingerprints, grid order, NOAA's mask rules, counts), and checks that stored temperature − difference − normal is exactly zero in every water cell.

Inputs (NOAA STAR file share, 9 Nov 2026): coraltemp_v3.1_20261109.nc (SHA-256 57de86e9…5b92), ct5km_ssta_v3.1-clim19912020-v1_20261109.nc (7b97760a…1033), ct5km_v3.1_clim-sst-mean-daily-window-01day-01grid-source19912020_day1109.nc (1495ac58…592e).

Credit and terms. Data: NOAA Coral Reef Watch CoralTemp v3.1 and SST anomaly, 1991–2020 baseline; credit and courtesy to NOAA Coral Reef Watch. The anomaly and climatology files state that Coral Reef Watch data are available for use without restriction, with credit. CoralTemp's own file also carries a Met Office notice labelled for its 1985–2002 data; no text we read extends it to the derived anomaly or climatology. Methods article DOI: 10.3390/rs12233856 (Skirving et al. 2020, Remote Sensing 12:3856) — an article, not a dataset DOI; NOAA lists no dataset DOI for these exact products. The files were downloaded from the server of the NOAA / NESDIS Center for Satellite Applications and Research (STAR), whose disclaimer allows free use with credit to STAR.

Microplastic samples — dated (observation, optional layer)

What it is. 1,145 surface-water samples taken with manta nets (0.33 mm mesh) by Oceaneye volunteers and analysed by Oceaneye staff, 2011–2024 (newest 23 Oct 2024), as published by NOAA NCEI in its Marine Microplastics collection (Accession 0276422). Each marker is one sample at the place the tow started, on its own sampling date. It is not where plastic is now, and it is one programme's routes — mostly Atlantic and Mediterranean — not global coverage. A missing marker does not establish plastic-free water. The layer is off when the page opens and is fetched only while switched on.

The number. Concentrations are shown in pieces per cubic metre (pieces smaller than 5 mm), as NOAA NCEI publishes them. NOAA warns that concentrations may not be comparable across studies because methods differ; this layer shows a single programme. NOAA's "Very Low … High" concentration classes are not shown: they are set from NOAA's whole database and shift as records are added, and on a globe they would read as danger levels.

Zero. A hollow ring marks a sample the source reports as zero: nothing counted in the sampled size range with this method on that tow. It says nothing about larger plastic or about other water.

Checking against the original. On every refresh the hub re-checks each published value from scratch against Oceaneye's original file (SEANOE 10.17882/86630, 2011–2020, CC BY 4.0), held once and pinned to its SHA-256. The calculation checked is: pieces counted in the < 5 mm size class ÷ (tow distance in km × 1,000 m/km × 0.60 m net width × 0.15 m sampled depth). Reproduced means the result is within 0.5 % of the published value — a check of that calculation only, not a quality grade. Nothing from an earlier check is carried forward: if NOAA changes a sample's date, position or value, the check runs again against the new values, and if the original file changes, every check pauses until it is reviewed. Measured on 4 Nov 2026: 647 reproduced; 11 have a possible corresponding original record with a different date (in each, the original record's end date equals NOAA's date; which is correct is not established); 1 could not be reproduced (the original record gives a tow distance of 0 m); 486 have no original record in the inspected file (472 dated after the file's last sample on 26 Nov 2020, 14 within its period). Every sample's own status is in its inspector.

Within the original file. Tow distance was measured by GPS (over ground) for some tows and by flowmeter for others; each checked sample shows which. The < 5 mm size class is recorded as "1 to 5 mm" for some samples and "0.3 to 5 mm" for others under the same 0.33 mm mesh — whether that is a labelling choice or a real lower bound is not established. Its values carry SeaDataNet quality flag 0 (no quality control recorded in that flag); Oceaneye states that its staff analysed all samples. Three samples appear in two regional files with identical values and are counted once.

Terms and citation. NOAA NCEI applies CC0 to its compiled database; Oceaneye's original file is CC BY 4.0; NCEI's archive record for the accession states no licence, recorded as unstated. Cite: NOAA NCEI Marine Microplastics — Nyadjro, E., et al. (2018), The NOAA NCEI Global Marine Microplastics Data Collection (1972-present), and Nyadjro, E. S., et al. (2023), Scientific Data 10, doi:10.1038/s41597-023-02632-y; dataset: Faure, F., Saini, C., Potter, G., Galgani, F., de Alencastro, L. F., Hagmann, P. Collection. Once a day the hub reads NOAA's published layer in pages of 1,000 and publishes only if the rows retrieved equal NOAA's own count, every row passes its checks (identifier, date, position, unit, value), and the total has not collapsed. A failed run keeps the previous file with its own times.

Orbital layers — computed positions (model tier)

Optional layers on World View, each off unless you turn it on, each touching only its own markers. Each marker is a position computed with the SGP4 model (satellite.js 6.0.2, pinned) from the latest general-perturbations element set Space-Track.org publishes for that object. Classification: model (propagated from published orbital elements — computed, not observed). They are not observation layers: they never enter the status report, the status sentence or search, and nothing about coverage, absence or change is read from them. (They replace the earlier Satellites overlay, which drew CelesTrak's "active" group.)

What they show. The scale and distribution of the public catalogue: every object Space-Track lists as not decayed whose elements are under 10 days old (Space-Track's recommended query). Older elements are filtered out upstream and are outside these counts. The layers follow Space-Track's object type — Payloads, Rocket bodies, Debris. Objects of unknown type are counted, not drawn. Payload operational status (active or inactive) is not used yet: a payload is shown as a payload, and whether it is working is not established here. When a status source is adopted the layers become Active satellites · Inactive satellites & rocket bodies · Orbital debris, and any payload without a usable status is counted, not drawn — missing status is never counted as inactive.

What they do not show. Not every object in orbit: classified objects and objects too small to track are not in the catalogue. Space surveillance networks track about 40,000 objects; ESA estimates more than 1.2 million objects larger than 1 cm (ESA Space Environment Report 2025, data to end of 2024). Not a measure of how much of the sky is obscured, of ground coverage, or of collision risk. Not an observed position: publicly released element sets are not suitable for conjunction assessment (Space-Track's own warning).

Always visible while any layer is on: "Markers enlarged for visibility; not to physical scale."; the time the positions were propagated for; when the element file was retrieved; for each layer that is on, the objects in the file, drawn and not drawn (unusable elements or propagation failure), how many use element sets older than three days at the time shown (a disclosure threshold, not an accuracy limit) and how many are higher than geostationary orbit (35,786 km); how many objects are counted but not drawn; and that completeness is unverified — Space-Track gives no row count, so a file cut short between rows could pass the hub's checks. If the latest refresh failed, the file still shown keeps its original retrieval time and the failure is stated; past 8 hours the file's age is stated.

Cadence and data path. The hub signs in to Space-Track with the project's own account and makes one trimmed request at :41 in odd UTC hours (two-hourly, off-peak, within Space-Track's limit of one such request an hour). It validates every row before publishing (18 fields, known object type, ISO epoch, unique catalogue number, final newline, row floors) and on any failure keeps the previous valid file with its own times. Your browser reads only the hub (a small description, the hub's last run, and the element file, checked against its published checksum) and never contacts Space-Track. While any layer is on, positions for the layers that are on are recomputed every 2 seconds in a background worker, only while the tab is visible; turning the last layer off stops the updates.

Source standing. Space-Track.org (User Agreement and "Redistribution of Basic SSA Information", read 2 Nov 2026): USSPACECOM gives an express blanket approval to redistribute basic SSA data — element sets included — accessed via www.Space-Track.org, conditioned on appropriate citation. Commercial use is not addressed. Credit: Space-Track.org; elements from the US Space Force 18th/19th Space Defense Squadrons.

Met catalogue sample — places of making (catalogued, optional card)

What it is. A fixed snapshot of what The Metropolitan Museum of Art's catalogue states about where some of its objects were made. 479 objects retained from a seeded 2,000-record sample of 59,137 Met records with typed geography. This filtered view draws from four departments; it does not represent the Met's whole collection or the world's art. It is a list on World View, off unless you switch it on; nothing is drawn on the globe. Classification: catalogued — a record of what an institution states, dated; not observed, not computed.

How the 479 were reached. The Met's MetObjects.csv at commit e394878 (source CSV of 2 Nov 2026) holds 503,073 records. 59,137 of them have a typed geography that maps to a relationship and a country, in five departments (Egyptian Art, Islamic Art, The American Wing, Medieval Art, The Cloisters). A seeded random sample of 2,000 was drawn from those (seed 20261107). Keeping only a certain catalogued place of making, aligned with its country and resolved to an ISO 3166-1 code, leaves 479 objects in four departments: The American Wing 326, Islamic Art 72, Medieval Art 43, The Cloisters 38. The view was prepared on 7 Nov 2026; the published file was generated later and carries its own generation time.

Arts of Africa, Oceania and the Americas is an excluded department, not an absence of those regions.

The Met's catalogued place of making, mapped to present-day countries — a location reference only, not historical nationality.

"Country of Origin" is its own relationship and is kept outside this view; superseded wording ("Formerly considered made in") never places an object. The Met's wording is shown verbatim.

Concentration: United States 239 of 479 (49.9%); 8 countries have one object; 12 have three or fewer.

Random page check: 0 of 27 reached Met pages disagree (95% interval for the error rate 0–12.5%); 23 pages were not reached and are unverified, not passes.

The Met's catalogue, not independent historical verification; the Met says cataloguing is ongoing.

Sensitivity not fully assessed: no object was manually assessed; 11 carry tentative labels; an unlabelled object is not established as not sensitive.

A fixed snapshot: the Met updates its CSV weekly; every figure here is tied to the source CSV of 2 Nov 2026 (commit e394878).

Sensitivity. Every object is marked "sensitivity not fully assessed". Eleven carry tentative labels (sacred/ritual 9, funerary 3, Indigenous source community 2; human remains 0) from keyword screens and three page findings; an unlabelled object is not established as not sensitive. Sensitive material is included with honest context for this metadata-only scope; inclusion is not a claim about ownership legitimacy, and source restrictions are kept.

Terms and credit. The Met releases its collection data under CC0 1.0: download, share, modify and distribute for any purpose, including commercial use, without permission. Attribution is not required; credit given: Collection data: The Metropolitan Museum of Art Open Access (CC0), source CSV of 2 Nov 2026 (commit e394878). Images are not part of the data and are not shown or fetched here; each object links to its page on the Met's site, which shows its own images under its own terms.

Data path. Your browser reads only the hub's /snapshot/arts. The hub serves the file only if the complete stored file matches the hash pinned in its code; otherwise the card says it is unavailable and why. The file never changes: fetching it again does not make the catalogue newer. It is not a collected layer and has no freshness grade.

The Sky on Two Clocks — Moon and Sun distances (computed, optional card)

What it is. One card with two views. Now shows how far the Moon and the Sun are from the centre of the Earth at 00:00 UTC today, with about 35 days (Moon) and one year (Sun) of the distance cycle around it. Deep Time explains how the orbits themselves change. Nothing is drawn on the globe unless you open the zoom-out view, which draws computed positions for the same instant (below).

Calculated, not observed. The distances are calculated by NASA/JPL Horizons (planetary ephemeris DE441): geometric, centre to centre, in kilometres, with Horizons' range rate (km/s) giving the direction. Mother Earth shows a dated daily sample from one export, refreshed by hand; the page never calls Horizons itself. Export: Moon and Sun, 2026-06-01 to 2027-12-31, retrieved 10 Nov 2026 (catalogue revision sky-2026-11-R1). A separate request for the Moon, using Horizons' TDB time scale and our conversion, agreed with the Moon export to within 0.17 m on every date.

Dates. "Today" is the UTC date on your device's clock, matched exactly: a day without its own sample shows as unavailable, and a neighbouring day is never shown in its place. From 4 Feb 2027 Horizons marks the times as estimates because future leap seconds are not yet known (IERS Bulletin C 72 rules one out only to the end of December 2026). A leap second at the end of June 2027 has not been decided; if one is added, samples from 1 Jul 2027 would be off by one second until the next refresh. The export is to be refreshed by 1 Jun 2027.

The Sun's annual range. Earth's orbit is an ellipse, so its distance from the Sun varies over a year, from about 147 to about 152 million km (NASA APOD, 4 Jan 2025, R. Nemiroff & J. Bonnell). In this export the closest daily sample is 147,104,596 km (3 Jan 2027) and the farthest 152,100,460 km (5 Jul 2027) — sampled extrema, not the instants of perihelion and aphelion.

Measured lunar recession. Lunar laser ranging shows the Moon's mean distance increasing by about 3.8 cm a year (Dickey et al. 1994, as given by NASA GSFC, Secular Acceleration of the Moon); the same page gives the lengthening of the day as about 2.3 milliseconds per century. The end of total solar eclipses: an estimate of about 650 million years, attributed to Lou Mayo, "The Very Last Solar Eclipse!", NASA eclipse2017 pages (the original page is no longer online; archived copy captured 4 Jan 2025); the article gives no source for the figure. The card says "hundreds of millions of years".

Estimated widening from solar mass loss — how the figure was traced. Genova et al. (2018) fitted a change in the Sun's gravitational parameter of (−6.13 ± 1.47) × 10⁻¹⁴ per year from MESSENGER tracking of Mercury, consistent with the Sun's expected mass loss (Nature Communications 9, 289; CC BY 4.0). If the change is slow, an orbit's size grows in proportion (a·GM ≈ constant, two-body approximation — Mother Earth's step, not stated in the paper): about 0.9 cm a year at Earth's distance (0.7–1.1 cm across the paper's uncertainty), roughly a metre per century at today's rate. NASA's article on the study quotes 1.5 cm per year per AU; that was the earlier theoretical prediction, which the measured value came in slightly below. Order of magnitude only. Over billions of years the ageing Sun sends Earth more energy (NASA Science).

Terms and credit. JPL Solar System Dynamics: "No permissions are needed. The data provided on this site are considered public domain." No NASA/Caltech/JPL endorsement is implied. Citation: NASA/JPL Solar System Dynamics. Data retrieved on 2026 November 10. Horizons System (API). https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov. Our modifications: daily 00:00 UTC samples selected; range and range rate kept as exported; values rounded for display.

Data path. Your browser reads only the hub's /catalogue/sky/sky-2026-11-R1. The hub serves it only if the complete stored file matches the hash pinned in its code, and this page checks the same hash again; otherwise the card says the distances are unavailable. The Deep Time view is part of this page and does not depend on that file.

Sky zoom-out view — Earth, Moon and Sun (computed, opened from the Two Clocks card)

What it is. From the Two Clocks card, "Zoom out to the Moon and Sun" pulls back from the globe you are looking at. The Moon comes into view, then, farther out, the Sun. Stop buttons go straight to Earth, Earth and Moon, or Earth, Moon and Sun; "Back to Earth" (or Esc) returns you exactly where you were. Nothing is fetched until you open it.

Computed, not observed. Positions are for 00:00 UTC on today's UTC date, the same daily sample as the distances card, from the same NASA/JPL Horizons export (DE441, geometric, Earth's centre). The distances in the labels are those same values. Horizons gives directions in the ICRF, a frame fixed to distant quasars; to place them over the globe we turn them to face the Earth's surface at that instant with IAU 2006 precession and Greenwich mean sidereal time. Assumptions: no nutation, polar motion taken as zero, UT1 taken as UTC, and the Earth-centred ICRF axes taken as the input frame of that calculation. On every date from 1 Jun 2026 to 31 Dec 2027 the result agrees within 0.0025° with the full IAU 2006/2000A calculation (ERFA), within 0.0028° with an independent library (astronomy-engine), and the Sun within 0.012° with this site's own day/night formula. That is agreement with references, not a claim of accuracy. IERS Bulletin A (5 Nov 2026) predicts UT1 − UTC within 0.27 s to 5 Nov 2027, an estimate; a difference of 0.3 s would move these points by about 0.0013°. Its predicted polar motion moves them by less than about 0.0002° (a sensitivity estimate).

Scale. Earth and Moon are drawn to scale: true sizes (Moon radius 1,737.4 km, IAU, from Horizons) and true distance. The Sun is drawn at 200 Earth radii instead of about 23,000, along its calculated direction, with its size set so that, as seen from Earth's centre, it covers the same angle as the real Sun (radius 695,700 km, IAU 2015, from Horizons). How big it looks on your screen depends on where the camera is. The text strip always shows the actual distances.

The point directly beneath each body. The text version gives, for each body, the calculated point on Earth directly beneath it at that instant (geocentric latitude and longitude). It is a way of stating the direction, not a place anything was seen from.

Layers and clocks. The globe's layers (quakes, fires, vessels, day/night, orbital objects and the rest) show their own times, not 00:00 UTC, so they are hidden while the view is open and come back exactly as they were. The Earth imagery keeps its own date, shown as usual. A day without its own sample shows "No computed position for today"; a neighbouring day is never used, and nothing is interpolated.

Code credit. The precession and sidereal-time code is transcribed from ERFA (a library derived from SOFA, rather than SOFA itself; BSD-3-Clause, © NumFOCUS Foundation), with its notices retained in the generator.

Data path. Your browser reads only the hub's /catalogue/sky/sky-scene-2026-11-R1, served only if the complete file matches its pin. The page checks the file's sha256 and shape, and that it names the distances card's own revision and hash as its pair; otherwise the view does not open.

Search — what it covers and what it cannot

The Explore panel on World View searches only the records your browser has already loaded from each layer's latest published snapshot. It issues no request of any kind: no geocoder, no place-name dataset, no country boundaries, no history. It is not a search of the world, and not a search of everything a source holds.

LayerWhat a search can match
EarthquakesThe description text exactly as USGS writes it (for example "14 km SSW of Volcano, Hawaii"), and coordinates.
Fire detectionsCoordinates only — FIRMS publishes no name or description for a detection, so it is never text-searched.
ShipsVessel name and MMSI as received in the sample, and coordinates.
Forest disturbanceNothing. This layer publishes counts per cell and no individual records.
Kp, CO₂, sea iceNothing. These are indices, not records with places.

A place or country word matches text, not territory. Searching "Chile" finds loaded records whose description contains that word and misses records in Chile described some other way. Nothing in this box knows where a border is.

Coordinates are entered latitude first, then longitude — decimal degrees, with optional hemisphere letters (-33.92, 18.42 or 33.92S, 18.42E). Latitude is accepted from −90 to 90 and longitude from −180 to 180; a value outside its range is refused with the range named. Leading decimals are read as written (.5 is half a degree, -.5 is half a degree south). A minus sign and a hemisphere letter together is ambiguous and is refused rather than guessed. A number that cannot be read whole is refused by name and never repaired — nothing is dropped from what you typed to make it parse, because a discarded character would silently move the position somewhere else. When both values are in range, a latitude and longitude typed the wrong way round cannot be detected, which is why the order is stated at the input.

A coordinate search always offers to move the view to that position, whether or not any record is near it. It then separately lists loaded records within 250 km, nearest first, up to twelve with the true total stated. That radius is a choice made for this box: it is not a coverage boundary and says nothing about what was observed there.

"No matching loaded records" describes the loaded snapshot and nothing else. It does not establish absence. A layer that is hidden, whose state is unknown, or whose records are withheld by the records-and-cells pairing rule is not searched at all, and the panel names each one with the reason.

A result stays tied to what it was found under. Every result carries its layer and the collector run it came from. If that run is replaced, or the layer is hidden, or the layer's state lapses to unknown, or its records are withheld, the result is marked and refused rather than opened — the page never attaches an old record to a newer run. Each result is judged on its own layer, so one layer refreshing does not invalidate another's results. Nothing moves the view and nothing is selected without a click. If this browser cannot show the globe there is no view to move, and the panel says so instead of reporting a movement that did not happen; searching and opening records still work.

Everything here — one place, all layers

Selecting any point on the globe (or "Everything we hold for this cell" in a cell's details) lists what the hub holds for that 4° grid cell, one layer per row. Each row keeps its own date, evidence and limits; the rows are listed together only because they share the cell — nothing in the list states a connection between them.

  • Any place can be selected, including one with no visible records. A layer with nothing for the cell says what that does and does not mean (e.g. "not a statement that none occurred", "not an empty sea") — never zero.
  • Records and counts come from the same collector run for each layer; if they do not, that layer is withheld with the reason, and the other layers are unaffected.
  • Optional layers that are off are not fetched. Each has a "Load this layer's data for this place" button; that fetches its data for the panel only — the layer stays off on the globe.
  • Collection evidence is labelled as such. "Our collector produced a usable sample" (all places at once) and "records for this cell reached us" (this place) are different statements, and neither is the source's geographic coverage. "Retained since" is when our record for that layer starts; "first retained report here" is the first retained hour with a record for this cell.
  • Cells differ in size: a 4° cell covers about 198,000 km² at the equator and far less toward the poles; the area is stated for each cell.
  • Kp, CO₂, sea ice, orbital objects and day/night have no per-place value and are listed with the reason.
