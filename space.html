How this image is made

A weather satellite about 36,000 km above the equator at 0° longitude scans the side of the Earth it faces, strip by strip, about every ten minutes. It is not a photograph taken in one instant.

EUMETSAT combines several of the instrument’s measurements into this “GeoColour” picture. Where it is day, visible-light measurements give colours close to what an eye would see. Where it is night, cloud is drawn from infrared measurements and laid over NASA’s Black Marble, a fixed map of city lights made on other dates. The lights do not show tonight’s lights, power cuts or fires.

What the time means. “Source frame time” is the time EUMETSAT’s map service names for the frame it served. Our hub publishes a picture only when the service itself confirms that time for it. We do not state when the scan started or finished.

What Mother Earth changed. We asked the service for this view (its own geostationary projection for this satellite position), this size (2,048 pixels square) and this surround colour, and received a JPEG. Nothing was recoloured, sharpened or retouched. Zooming on this page enlarges that same picture.

How it gets here. Our hub looks for a newer frame every ten minutes, checks the picture it receives before keeping it, and serves it to this page. This page never contacts EUMETSAT. If a check or a collection fails, the last verified picture stays and the lines above the picture say what happened.
